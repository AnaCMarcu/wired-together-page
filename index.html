Wired Together

Learning Persistent Relationships through Reward-Modulated Social Plasticity

Agents learn not only how to cooperate, but whom to cooperate with.

We model a team of agents as an adaptive social network. Each agent holds a directed bond to every teammate; social co-activity marks a pair as eligible for change, and later outcomes consolidate or weaken the bond. The learned graph then feeds back into how agents learn and whom they turn to when they deliberate.

The central idea

A social graph learned from interaction and reward

Coordination in multi-agent systems is typically specified per task, by a fixed topology, a dialogue protocol, or a planner that assigns work. We study a second axis of design: with whom agents have a relationship, learned online and kept.

  1. 1

    Interact

    Agents communicate, observe and imitate one another, and work close together while engaged. Each such event is a directed co-firing cij(t) that marks the pair as eligible to change.

    Figure panels (a)–(b)
  2. 2

    Consolidate

    Reward-modulated plasticity updates the directed bond Wij(t). An eligibility trace carries recent co-firing forward, so a later reward strengthens the bond, failure weakens it, and every bond slowly decays unless renewed.

    Panels (c)–(d)
  3. 3

    Use

    The graph W(t) shapes what agents do next: during RL fine-tuning through reward diffusion and bond-gated experience sharing, and at inference time through bond-conditioned social deliberation.

    Panel (e)

Key findings

What the learned graph changes

Three headline results from WIRE, each against a matched baseline without the social graph. Teams of three unless stated, 1,000-step episodes, mean ± SD across seeds.

RQ1 · RL fine-tuning

Social plasticity improves cooperation in three of four RL fine-tuning configurations.

When the learned graph shapes learning, through reward diffusion and bond-gated experience sharing, per-agent cooperative completion rises for Qwen3.5-2B under IPPO and MAPPO and for Gemma-E4B under IPPO, where it more than quadruples (0.8% → 3.5%). Gemma-E4B MAPPO is the exception (2.2% → 0.4%).

Seed-to-seed spread is large: the whiskers span ±1 SD.

RQ1 · Against central orchestration

Social plasticity outperforms soft and hard central orchestration at inference time on Gemma-E4B.

It reaches a task return of 548, against 505 for a soft (VillagerAgent-style) orchestrator and 477 for a hard (HMAS-2) one, and a cooperative completion of 4.8% against 4.7% and 3.9%. It does so at the soft orchestrator's inference compute and a third of the hard orchestrator's.

At inference time the benefit is backbone-dependent: it does not transfer uniformly to the Qwen backbones.

Test-time scaling · 3 to 15 agents

Learned relationships strengthen emergent structure and cooperation as teams grow.

Scaling zero-shot Gemma-E4B teams from 3 to 15 agents raises budget-averaged team cooperative completion from 9.6% to 18.3% without any social graph, a gain that comes with team size, not with plasticity. On top of it, social plasticity adds 1.3–3.5 points at every team size except N = 10, where the arms tie, and forms tighter conversational groups than the baseline at every size. Its largest gain comes with the smallest message budget (+2.9 points at 600 tokens per agent).

Team completion counts each milestone once per team, so it is not comparable with the per-agent rates above.

Emergent organisation · fifteen agents

Coordination emerges without assigned roles

Fifteen zero-shot Gemma-E4B agents receive the same prompt apart from their names, and every message is addressed to exactly one teammate. Nobody is given a role, yet a focal agent becomes the team's hub and the others split into small conversational groups.

A focal hub

At N = 15 the focal agent talks with about ten distinct partners, the others with about three. It gains most of them in Chambers 1–2.

Small conversational groups

Each agent keeps a few frequent partners, so larger teams form more groups rather than larger ones: 3.4–3.9 groups per episode at N = 15.

Tighter with social plasticity

At N = 15, grouping beyond chance is +0.22 with social plasticity against +0.13 for the baseline, and social plasticity forms tighter groups at every team size.

Paper

Abstract, code and citation

Abstract

Coordination in multi-agent systems is typically specified per task, by a fixed topology, a dialogue protocol, or a planner that assigns work. We study a second axis of design: with whom agents have a relationship. We model a multi-agent system as an adaptive social network whose directed bonds evolve online through reward-modulated social plasticity: social co-activity marks a pair as eligible, and later outcomes consolidate or weaken the bond. The learned graph feeds back into learning, through reward diffusion and bond-gated experience sharing, and into inference, through social deliberation.

In WIRE, an embodied environment of five chambers with increasing coordination demands, relational coupling improves cooperative completion in three of four RL fine-tuning configurations, and at inference time outperforms both a soft and a hard centralised orchestrator, the latter at a third of its compute. Communication builds the strongest bonds, while observation and imitation yield the highest cooperative completion, and retaining only bonds after team recomposition balances exploiting past partners with exploring new ones. Scaling teams from 3 to 15 agents raises budget-averaged team cooperative completion from 9.6% to 18.3%, and to 19.6% with social plasticity, and structured communication develops without assigned roles: small conversational groups, a focal agent, and coordination beyond immediate neighbours. Social plasticity raises cooperative progress further, especially under limited communication budgets. Learned relationships thus offer a decentralised, inspectable complement to orchestration.
